Decided · the rally mark · ADR-0011

Five ways to mark a rally

The competitor flashes the field green. Their field is black and carries no information, so green is free. Ours is a court whose two halves already mean something, and once the themes arrive those halves are clay, grass or hard court. Five candidates were drawn and fired across four surfaces. The chosen one is D — the half’s own tint lifts. The other four are kept here because a decision without its alternatives is not a decision, it is an assertion. The thing itself is called the rally mark: a rally is the unit of play, and a point is only its result.

Read against a court that no longer ships. Every surface fired at below is the two-tone court with its three painted lines, and court-surface has since collapsed that into one blue, #17406F, with no lines on it at all. The decision stands and gets easier — D's per-surface strength becomes one number rather than one per half — but candidate B is now unbuildable rather than merely rejected: there are no painted lines left to flare. It is kept anyway, because a rejected alternative is the record of an argument and not a proposal.

The sweep

What the sweep shows

The chosen one first, then the four it was chosen over. Read against ADR-0006, which is the constraint none of them gets to spend.

D · The tint lifts chosen

the half’s own hue, screened

The half that won brightens in its own colour and falls back. It reads as the court itself responding rather than as something laid over it, and it adds no token to the palette — nothing for ADR-0006 to object to, because there is no second accent to argue with the first.

What it costs: the mark has no colour of its own, so strength is a property of the surface, not of the mark. The same 0.34 that reads clearly on turf green is nearly gone on glass blue, and every theme shipped afterwards names its own number. That is a field on a theme, and it is the price of the option that spends nothing.

A · Burst of light

floodlight #FFF8D6

The primitive already exists, already has tests, and its doc comment has pre-argued the case: “a light and not a hue — the moment this reads as a second accent it is too strong, and the fix is strength, never a warmer colour.” Louder than D and identical on every surface, since warm white does not care what it falls on. The runner-up, and where to go if D turns out to be too quiet on a wrist at arm’s length.

B · The paint flares

courtLine at full weight

The service line, centre line and outline go to full white and fade back to their 0.20–0.36 weights. Theme-proof for the same reason as A — court paint is white on clay, grass and hard alike — and it never touches the digit. But it is quiet: from a bench six metres away, a hairline is not what the eye catches.

C · Wash of ball yellow

ball #DDF35C

Defensible in one word — a rally won is “chosen”. But yellow already means the serve on this exact screen, and a yellow bloom over the opponents’ half says “this is yours” about a rally that was not ours. On clay it also collides with the surface underneath it.

E · Green, as they do it

a second accent

Costs ADR-0006 outright: “once loss is red, the yellow stops meaning ‘yours’ and starts meaning ‘good’.” Then the sweep kills it twice more — on Wimbledon grass a green flash on green is nearly invisible, and on Roland Garros clay it is the one hue that has no business being there.

What the decision carries with it

Strength becomes a property of the surface. D has no colour of its own, so it cannot have one number. Whatever describes a theme — the two half tints, the paint, the ink — grows a field for how far that surface lifts when a rally lands on it. The sliders above are where each one gets settled, by eye, on the theme it belongs to.

The halves stop being told apart by hue. On real clay both halves are clay; on grass both are grass. Turf-green-against-glass-blue is the app’s entire answer to “which half is ours” today — ScoreView is explicit that it is never said by a label, because a label would take room from the digit the watch is being looked at for. Under themes that answer degrades to a difference in value. It is the theme feature’s problem rather than this one’s, but D lives directly on top of it: the mark and the identity of the half are now made of the same material.

An undo is not marked. The obvious move was the mark reversed — the half starts lit and drains rather than blooming — and the stage above still carries the button so it can be checked. It does not survive the check: the only difference between the two is a 73ms rise inside a 520ms mark, and an eye does not read that as a different event. Marking an undo the same way a rally is marked is worse than not marking it, so it is left unmarked. If it turns out to matter — an undo done on a wrist is invisible to four people reading the phone on the bench — the answer is a different mechanism, not a reversed one.

Nothing here has to be drawn twice. The mark is geometry-free — a half brightening does not care that the phone’s board is landscape with the net turned a quarter turn. Unlike serveAlignment(for:from:), which needs writing once per screen, this is written once.
