# The app speaks two languages, and English is the source

The glossary used to say the app's strings were Russian and were not to be translated. They are now Russian and English both, and the reader's phone chooses between them: v1 is going to the App Store, and an app that speaks one language is invisible to everyone outside one market. English is the source — the literal standing in the code is the English sentence and the key its Russian translation is found by, at once — because the code and everything written around it is English already, and a key in one language inside a file written in another is a thing to hold in your head for no return.

## Considered options

**Russian literals as keys** would have been the smaller change: what is in the code stays where it is, and English arrives as the translation. Rejected because it leaves the source written in two languages at once, and because the project's development region is `en` — the catalog's source language would then disagree with the project's own.

**Symbolic keys** (`history.empty.title`) tell key and text apart properly, and cost the reader the sentence: every screen in the source stops saying what it says, and a preview shows a key until the catalog resolves it. That is a fair price in an app with a dozen languages. Not in one with two.

## Consequences

- **The plural forms belong to the catalog, not to us.** Russian has four and English two, and `MatchWording` held a hand-written table for the Russian ones plus a special case for the genitive that "до" governs — "до 21 очка", but "до 22 очков". Each key now carries its own forms per language, which is where that special case stops being special: it is simply what that key says for that category.
- **The pinned locale is gone.** Dates and durations were formatted through a hard-coded `ru_RU` for one reason, written down in the code: with no second language, an English phone would have put "1 hr, 35 min" into the middle of a Russian row. With two, `Locale.current` gives the app's language and the reader's own region — a Russian speaker in Spain gets Russian words and the local order of the day and the month.
- **One catalog, two targets.** `Shared/Localizable.xcstrings` is a member of both. Where the phone and the watch genuinely say the same thing — "Us", "Opponents" — sharing it is simply right. Where they do not, the rule that they say different things survives as a rule only: the friction that used to enforce it was two separate sets of literals, and that is gone. The next reader has to be told so in as many words.
- **One English sentence can carry two meanings.** With the sentence as the key, two meanings that happen to share it collide into a single Russian translation. The sanctioned escape is `String(localized:)` with an explicit `key:` and a `defaultValue:` — two keys, the same English text. Reach for that rather than bending one of the two sentences until it stops colliding.
- **The phone chooses the language, and there is no picker.** Declaring two localizations gets a per-app language switch in Settings for free; one of our own would be another thing to walk past before a match.
- **The engine stays mute.** `PadelScoring`, `PadelStorage` and `PadelDelivery` hold no user-facing string and are to hold none: they hand over data, and the targets say the sentences. "40" and "AD" are the exception that proves it — padel's own notation, not English, and not translated.
- **The English is American, and so is every other word written here.** A tiebreak is one word and a color has no "u", in the strings and in the prose around them alike: the repo's own writing was British and was converted in the same breath. The split — American strings, British prose — was the cheaper option and was rejected, because the prose sits directly above the strings. A doc comment reading "our side's color" is the example the next string gets written from, and a rule that has to be remembered instead of seen is a rule that lasts until the first tired afternoon. Apple's vocabulary is American already — `String(localized:)`, `Localizable.xcstrings`, "development localization" — so the platform was never the thing disagreeing.
- **The source language is `en`, not `en-US`.** An English phone anywhere in the world should match it, and there is no second English here to tell it apart from. American is simply what that `en` is written in.
- **Every new string is two strings now, and stays two forever.** That is the price of the decision and the reason it is hard to reverse: shipping English means keeping English.
